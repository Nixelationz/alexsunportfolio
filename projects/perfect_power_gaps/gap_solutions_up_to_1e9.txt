ALL CONSECUTIVE PERFECT-POWER GAPS OF SIZE 1 THROUGH 100
A finite computational census up to 10^9

Abstract

We enumerate every pair of consecutive distinct perfect powers whose
difference is at most 100 and whose larger member is at most 10^9. For each
gap size n from 1 to 100, the census gives the number of such pairs and lists
every pair. The search finds 114 pairs, representing 73 distinct gap sizes.
The remaining 27 gap sizes have no such pair within this bound. These are
finite-search results and do not assert that a missing gap is impossible.

1. Definitions and scope

A perfect power is an integer of the form a^k, where a and k are integers
with a >= 2 and k >= 2. Equal values with different power representations
are treated as one perfect power. Write the distinct perfect powers in
increasing order as

    p_1 < p_2 < p_3 < ... .

For each integer n with 1 <= n <= 100, we count the indices i such that

    p_(i+1) <= 10^9  and  p_(i+1) - p_i = n.

Thus a solution is an adjacent pair in the complete ordered list of
distinct perfect powers, not merely any two powers with difference n. The
lower and upper values are both at most 10^9. Every solution is printed
below as a difference of powers and as a difference of integers. Each value
is given one convenient reduced power representation; equivalent spellings
of the same value are not counted as additional solutions.

2. Enumeration method

For every prime exponent q <= floor(log_2(10^9)), the search generates all
integer powers b^q <= 10^9 for b >= 2. Every perfect power occurs among
these generated values: if its exponent is composite, it has a prime
divisor, and the base can be raised accordingly. The values are deduplicated
and sorted; adjacent entries are scanned and differences from 1 through
100 are recorded.

This procedure yields 32,669 distinct perfect-power values up to 10^9.
Scanning adjacent values gives 114 qualifying pairs in total. The census is
exhaustive for the stated bound and gap range.

5. Remarks

The zero counts mean only that the exhaustive enumeration found no adjacent
pair for that gap with upper value at most 10^9. No conclusion is drawn about
solutions beyond 10^9. The gap sizes are restricted to 1 through 100; larger
differences are outside the scope of this census.

6. Additional restrictions reported for the 27 zero-count gaps

The missing gaps in this census are

    6, 14, 22, 29, 31, 34, 42, 44, 46, 50, 52, 54, 58, 62,
    64, 66, 70, 72, 78, 82, 84, 86, 88, 90, 91, 96, 98.

The restrictions below are stronger than the finite bound 10^9: they concern
specified forms of a hypothetical solution, not just the pairs found by
enumeration.

6.1 Excluding square and cube endpoint families

The reported restrictions are:

  (i) None of the 27 gaps is realized by two consecutive perfect powers
      that are both squares. For even gaps this follows because the
      difference of two squares is odd. For the odd missing gaps 29, 31,
      and 91, the reported analysis says every candidate square interval
      contains another perfect power.

 (ii) None is realized by consecutive perfect powers that are a square and
      a cube. The associated equations are

          Y^2 = X^3 + n    or    Y^2 = X^3 - n.

      The supplied summary reports that the integral-point cases have been
      classified using complete integral-point results, and that any
      positive solutions do not give consecutive perfect powers.

(iii) The only missing gaps reported as expressible as a difference of two
      positive cubes are 91 and 98; both such pairs have an intervening
      perfect power. Thus none of the 27 gaps is realized by consecutive
      powers that are both cubes.

The classification statements in (ii) are reported results, not
re-established by the enumeration in this document. The source information
provided for them ("Cambridge University Press+2") is not a complete
bibliographic citation; a publication title, authors, and precise theorem
or data reference should be supplied before treating this document as a
fully sourced account of those classifications.

6.2 Consequence for exponents

If a hypothetical solution for one of these gaps had both exponents with
only 2 and 3 as prime divisors, each endpoint would be a square or a cube.
The exclusions above would rule out every such pairing. Accordingly, under
the reported exclusions, at least one of the exponents b,d must have a
prime divisor p >= 5. Equivalently, at least one endpoint has a
p-th-power representation for some prime p >= 5.

This leaves families such as

    x^2 - y^5 = +/- n
    x^3 - y^5 = +/- n
    x^2 - y^7 = +/- n

and, more generally, differences of powers with at least one exponent
having a prime divisor at least 5. Equations involving fifth powers can
lead to genus-2 curves, for which determining all integral points is
substantially harder than solving the corresponding Mordell equations.

6.4 Status of the restrictions

The reported work excludes square-square, square-cube, and cube-cube
realizations for all 27 missing gap sizes. It does not prove any of the 27
gaps impossible. In particular, the statement that a prime divisor at
least 5 must occur in one exponent is conditional on the reported family
exclusions above; it is not a conclusion of the 10^9 enumeration alone.
The Mordell-equation summary reports that neither sign has integral points
for 16 gaps, one sign has points for 7 gaps, and both signs have points for
4 gaps. These equation-level point counts do not, by themselves, determine
whether a consecutive-perfect-power solution exists.
